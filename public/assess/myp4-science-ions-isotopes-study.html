⚛️ Ions

Understanding how atoms gain or lose electrons to form charged particles (Haese Science Chapter 4)

What are Ions?

An ion is an atom or molecule with a net electrical charge due to the loss or gain of electrons.

Neutral Atom → Ion + e⁻ (or Ion - e⁻)
Cation: positive ion (lost electrons)
Anion: negative ion (gained electrons)
Remember: Ions are formed to achieve a stable electron configuration (usually 8 valence electrons - the octet rule).

Formation of Ions

Atoms gain or lose electrons to achieve stable electron configurations:

Na → Na⁺ + e⁻
Cl + e⁻ → Cl⁻

📝 Example 1: Sodium Ion

Neutral Sodium (Na): 11 protons, 11 electrons

Sodium Ion (Na⁺): 11 protons, 10 electrons

Charge: +1 (one more proton than electrons)

📝 Example 2: Magnesium Ion Formation

Step 1: Neutral Mg has 12 protons and 12 electrons (2,8,2 configuration)

Step 2: Loses 2 valence electrons from outer shell

Result: Mg²⁺ with 12 protons, 10 electrons (2,8 configuration - stable!)

🧬 Isotopes

Atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons (Haese Science Chapter 4)

What are Isotopes?

Isotopes are variants of a chemical element that have the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons.

Same: Atomic number (protons)
Different: Mass number (protons + neutrons)
Key Point: Isotopes of an element have identical chemical properties but different physical properties (like density).

Notation

Isotopes are represented using mass number and atomic number:

\(^{A}_{Z}X\)

X = element symbol, Z = atomic number, A = mass number

📝 Example 3: Carbon Isotopes

Carbon-12: \(^{12}_{6}C\) (6p⁺, 6n⁰)

Carbon-13: \(^{13}_{6}C\) (6p⁺, 7n⁰)

Carbon-14: \(^{14}_{6}C\) (6p⁺, 8n⁰)

Calculating Neutrons

Number of neutrons = Mass number (A) − Atomic number (Z)

📝 Example 4: Oxygen-17

Notation: \(^{17}_{8}O\)

Protons: 8

Neutrons: 17 - 8 = 9

Electrons: 8 (neutral atom)

Radioactive Isotopes

Some isotopess are unstable and undergo radioactive decay:

📝 Example 5: Carbon-14 Dating

Use: Dating archaeological artifacts

Half-life: 5,730 years

Process: C-14 decays to nitrogen-14

🌍 Real-World: Medical Isotopes

Iodine-131: Used to treat thyroid cancer (radioactive isotope)

Technetium-99m: Used in 80% of all nuclear medicine scans

Key Point: Only the nucleus changes during radioactivity - electron configuration stays the same!

⚡ Ionic Bonding

Transfer of electrons between atoms to form oppositely charged ions (Haese Science Chapter 5)

What is Ionic Bonding?

Ionic bonding is the electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions formed by electron transfer.

Metal + Non-metal → Ionic Compound
Electron transfer: Metal loses, Non-metal gains
Remember: Ionic bonds typically form between metals (left side of periodic table) and non-metals (right side).

Formation of Sodium Chloride (NaCl)

📝 Example 6: NaCl Formation

Step 1: Na atom loses 1 electron → Na⁺

Step 2: Cl atom gains 1 electron → Cl⁻

Step 3: Na⁺ and Cl⁻ attract → NaCl (table salt)

Electron transfer: 1 electron moves from Na to Cl

Dot-and-Cross Diagrams

Visual representation of electron transfer:

Na• + •Cl: → Na⁺ [:Cl:]⁻

Dots = Na electrons, Crosses = Cl electrons

📝 Example 7: Magnesium Oxide (MgO)

Mg: 2,8,2 → loses 2 electrons → Mg²⁺

O: 2,6 → gains 2 electrons → O²⁻

Formula: MgO (1:1 ratio)

Formula Writing

Balance charges to determine the formula:

Ca²⁺ + Cl⁻ → CaCl₂
Al³⁺ + O²⁻ → Al₂O₃

📝 Example 8: Calcium Chloride

Ca²⁺ needs 2 Cl⁻ to balance charge

Total positive: +2

Total negative: 2 × (-1) = -2

Formula: CaCl₂

💎 Properties of Ionic Compounds

How ionic bonding affects physical properties (Haese Science Chapter 5)

🔥 High Melting Point

Strong electrostatic forces between ions require lots of energy to overcome.

NaCl: 801°C
MgO: 2852°C

⚡ Electrical Conductivity

Conduct electricity when molten or dissolved (ions can move).

Solid: ❌ No conduction
Liquid: ✅ Conduction

💧 Solubility

Many ionic compounds dissolve in water (polar solvent).

NaCl: Soluble
CaCO₃: Insoluble

📝 Example 9: Comparing MgO and NaCl

MgO: Mg²⁺ and O²⁻ → 2+ and 2- charges → stronger attraction → higher melting point (2852°C)

NaCl: Na⁺ and Cl⁻ → 1+ and 1- charges → weaker attraction → lower melting point (801°C)

Rule: Higher ion charges = stronger ionic bonds = higher melting point!

🌍 Real-World Applications

How ions, isotopess and ionic bonding are used in everyday life

🧂 Table Salt (NaCl)

Essential for human health - nerves need Na⁺ ions to transmit signals. Too much causes high blood pressure.

💊 Medicine

Isotope iodine-131 treats thyroid issues. Ionic compounds like Mg²⁺ help muscle function.

🕰️ Carbon Dating

Carbon-14 isotopess date archaeological finds up to 50,000 years old. Used on Dead Sea Scrolls!

🔋 Batteries

Lithium-ion batteries use Li⁺ movement between electrodes. Ionic conductivity is key!

💎 Gemstones

Different ions give gemstones their colors: Cr³⁺ = ruby red, Co²⁺ = blue glass.

🏗️ Construction

Calcium ions (Ca²⁺) in cement and concrete. Strong ionic bonds = strong buildings!

🌍 Swedish Example: Vattenfall Power Plants

Context: Swedish power company uses ionic compounds in batteries for renewable energy storage.

Science: Lithium-ion (Li⁺) and sodium-ion (Na⁺) batteries store energy from wind/solar for later use.

Sweden aims for 100% renewable electricity by 2040 - ionic battery tech is crucial!
